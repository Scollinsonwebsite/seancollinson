Everybody says they want peace. Then exchange day comes, the phone lights up at 11 p.m., and a different brain takes over.

Scoring:

* Minus scores are narcissist pattern thinking: ego, control, winning, retaliation, blame, punishment.

5. The 25 questions
Question 1. The 11 p.m. text
It is 11 p.m. The other parent texts: "You never let me see the kids. My lawyer will hear about this." Your first move?

* Minus 2: Fire back a list of everything they have done wrong, so the record is clear tonight.
* Minus 1: Send one sharp reply so they know you are not scared, then try to sleep.
* Plus 1: Do not answer tonight. Read it again in the morning with clear eyes.
* Plus 2: Answer briefly in the morning: name what they want (more time), and offer a time to talk about a schedule that gets them there.

Question 2. Exchange day, 20 minutes late
At pickup, the other parent is 20 minutes late for the third time this month. The kids are watching from the doorway.

* Minus 2: Let them hear it in the driveway. The kids should know who keeps messing this up.
* Minus 1: Say nothing out loud, but make sure your face and your silence punish them.
* Plus 1: Keep the exchange warm and short for the kids, and raise the late pattern later, in writing.
* Plus 2: Keep it warm, then propose a fix that removes the fight: a set exchange window and a text if either of you runs more than 10 minutes behind.

Question 3. The court threat
In the middle of an argument about the holiday schedule, the other parent says: "Fine, we will let a judge decide."

* Minus 2: "Good. A judge will finally see what you really are."
* Minus 1: Threaten back with the things you could file, so they back down first.
* Plus 1: Slow it down. Ask what part of the schedule they actually cannot live with.
* Plus 2: Name the cost out loud, calmly: court takes a year and a fortune, and a stranger decides. Then put two workable holiday options on the table.

Question 4. The kids as messengers
Your 9 year old says: "Mom says you do not pay enough, so that is why we cannot do soccer."

* Minus 2: Set the record straight with your child right now, in detail, with numbers.
* Minus 1: Tell your child the other parent is lying, but keep it short.
* Plus 1: Keep your child out of the money fight: "That is a grown up topic, and both of us are handling it." Raise it with the other parent directly.
* Plus 2: Do that, and then propose a written rule you both follow: money and court topics never travel through the kids, and a shared note for kid related expenses so there is nothing to argue through them.

Question 5. Support money fight
Child support lands three days late, again. Rent is due.

* Minus 2: Withhold the next exchange until the money shows up. Consequences teach.
* Minus 1: Send a message designed to shame them into paying on time from now on.
* Plus 1: Send one factual message: amount, date due, date received, and ask what changed.
* Plus 2: Do that, and propose structure that ends the monthly fight: automatic transfer on a fixed date, with a written plan for the rare month it slips.

Question 6. The school play
Both of you are at the school play. The other parent sits two rows ahead and does not acknowledge you. Your child keeps looking back and forth.

* Minus 2: Make a comment your child can hear. Kids deserve to know who the bigger person is not.
* Minus 1: Spend the show composing the text you will send about respect.
* Plus 1: Watch your child, wave when they look, and let the seating be nothing.
* Plus 2: Watch your child, and afterward send one line: "She lit up seeing both of us there." Build the next event on that.

Question 7. A new partner appears
The other parent introduces a new partner at a game, with no warning to you first.

* Minus 2: Pull the other parent aside at the game. This gets handled now, in front of whoever hears it.
* Minus 1: Interrogate your kids afterward about the new partner, thoroughly.
* Plus 1: Be pleasant in the moment, feel your feelings later, and ask for a heads up next time, privately.
* Plus 2: Do that, and suggest you both follow the same simple rule: new partners get mentioned to the other parent before events, so the kids never have to manage adult surprises.

Question 8. Homework at the other house
Your child says homework never gets done at the other parent's house, and a project is now late.

* Minus 2: Document it for court and tell the other parent the file is growing.
* Minus 1: Fix it yourself at midnight and add it to the list of things you do that they do not.
* Plus 1: Ask the other parent what homework time looks like at their house before assuming the worst.
* Plus 2: Propose one shared system: the same homework window both houses, one shared folder or app, so the child carries the routine instead of the conflict.

Question 9. A medical decision
Your child needs braces. The other parent says no, it is cosmetic and too expensive, end of discussion.

* Minus 2: Book it anyway and send them half the bill. Forgiveness over permission.
* Minus 1: Tell them a real parent would not cheap out on their own kid.
* Plus 1: Ask the orthodontist for the medical reasoning and costs in writing, and share the facts, not your opinion of them.
* Plus 2: Share the facts, ask what their actual concern is (cost, timing, the treatment itself), and trade on that interest: payment split over time, a second opinion together, or a start date that works.

Question 10. Read receipts
You send a reasonable message about the weekend. It shows as read. Six hours pass. No reply.

* Minus 2: Send follow ups until you get one. Silence is an answer you refuse to accept.
* Minus 1: Screenshot the read receipt. It may matter someday, and they should know you keep them.
* Plus 1: Let it sit. One clear message does not need a chase, and the weekend plan was already in writing.
* Plus 2: Let it sit, and at the next calm moment agree on a simple reply window you both follow, like 24 hours for non emergencies, so silence stops being a weapon either of you has to read into.

Question 11. The family group chat
The other parent's sibling posts something in the family chat that paints you as the problem in the divorce.

* Minus 2: Correct the record in the chat, with receipts, tonight.
* Minus 1: Send the sibling a private message warning them to stay out of it.
* Plus 1: Stay out of the chat. That audience cannot rule on your divorce, and your kids may read it someday.
* Plus 2: Stay out, and put your energy where rulings actually happen: a clear written agreement and calm, documented communication with the other parent.

Question 12. The attorney letter
A formal letter from the other parent's attorney arrives, full of accusations you believe are exaggerated.

* Minus 2: Call the other parent directly and let them know exactly what you think of it.
* Minus 1: Write your own point by point rebuttal tonight and send it before you cool down.
* Plus 1: Read it twice, note the two or three claims that are actually decisions to make, and ignore the adjectives.
* Plus 2: Do that, respond in writing only to the decision points, and propose mediation on those points before either side spends more on letters.

Question 13. Your child asks the question
Your child asks: "Why can I not just live with you all the time?"

* Minus 2: Finally, honesty: explain the reasons, including what the other parent does wrong.
* Minus 1: Say "ask your mother" or "ask your father," and let them carry it.
* Plus 1: Protect both doors: "You have two homes and two parents who love you. If the schedule is hard, tell me the hard part."
* Plus 2: Do that, write down the hard part they named, and bring that specific problem, not a custody war, to the other parent or a mediator to solve.

Question 14. The schedule swap request
The other parent asks to swap weekends for a family wedding, three days before your weekend.

* Minus 2: No. The order is the order. They would never do it for you.
* Minus 1: Agree, but attach a condition that settles an old score.
* Plus 1: Check your real plans first. If it costs you little, say yes cleanly, in writing.
* Plus 2: Say yes cleanly, confirm the swap and the makeup time in one short written message, and bank the goodwill. Flexibility you give on paper is flexibility you can ask for later.

Question 15. Social media
A friend tags you in a post joking about your ex. It is funny, and it is a little mean.

* Minus 2: Share it. After everything, you have earned one public laugh.
* Minus 1: Like it and add a comment that says plenty without saying it.
* Plus 1: Scroll past. Anything public lives forever, and your kids will find it.
* Plus 2: Scroll past, and ask the friend privately to keep you out of posts about your ex. Protecting your kids' image of both parents is part of the job.

Question 16. In the mediation room
In mediation, the other parent cries, then accuses you of never caring about the family.

* Minus 2: Correct the history, loudly enough that the mediator hears the real story.
* Minus 1: Go cold and silent. If they want a performance, they can have an audience of one.
* Plus 1: Let the mediator run the room. Breathe, take notes, answer the proposal, not the accusation.
* Plus 2: Use the negotiator move: label it without heat. "It sounds like you felt alone in this for a long time." Watch the temperature drop, then return to the decision on the table.

Question 17. The vacation request
You want to take the kids on a trip that runs two days into the other parent's time. You are filling out the request.

* Minus 2: Book it first. It is easier to defend a done deal than to ask permission.
* Minus 1: Ask, but frame it so a no makes them look like the parent who ruined a vacation.
* Plus 1: Ask early, with dates, flight details, and where the kids will be each night.
* Plus 2: Ask early with full details, and offer the two days back plus first choice of makeup dates. Make yes the easiest answer they give all month.

Question 18. The report card
Grades slip this semester, and the drop is mostly on the other parent's days, as far as you can tell.

* Minus 2: Send the report card with your conclusion attached: their house, their fault.
* Minus 1: Raise it at the next exchange, in the driveway, so it does not get forgotten.
* Plus 1: Ask the teacher what changed, in the classroom, before assigning blame to a household.
* Plus 2: Bring the teacher's facts to a short, scheduled call with the other parent, framed around one shared interest you both actually hold: this kid, back on track, this semester.

Question 19. The apology that never comes
You are certain you are owed an apology for something the other parent did during the marriage. It has never come.

* Minus 2: Keep it on the table in every negotiation until it does. Some things should cost them.
* Minus 1: Bring it up whenever talks stall, as a reminder of who started this.
* Plus 1: Notice the hold it has on your decisions, and refuse to let one missing sentence run your settlement.
* Plus 2: Do the Reset move: write down what the apology would actually change about your future, then negotiate for that future directly. Peace you can build beats an apology you cannot force.

Question 20. Winning, honestly
Be honest. If a judge ruled tomorrow and you got everything you asked for, but the other parent was humiliated in the process, how would that sit with you?

* Minus 2: Fine. Better than fine. They had it coming.
* Minus 1: It would not be the goal, but it would not keep you up at night either.
* Plus 1: Uneasy. My kids have to live inside whatever this fight does to both of us.
* Plus 2: That is not a win to me anymore. A win is an agreement my kids can grow up inside without picking a side.

Question 21. The teacher's email
Your child's teacher emails both of you: your child has been distracted and acting out in class this month. What is your first move?

* Minus 2: Reply, copying the other parent, with your theory of what goes on at their house.
* Minus 1: Forward it to your attorney. A paper trail on their parenting is never wasted.
* Plus 1: Reply to the teacher only, asking what she is seeing and what would help, before looping in blame.
* Plus 2: Do that, then propose a short call with the other parent and one shared message to the school: one plan, both houses, focused on the child in front of the teacher.

Question 22. The birthday party
The other parent plans your child's birthday party on your weekend, invites your whole side of the family, and tells you after the invitations are out.

* Minus 2: Plan your own bigger party the weekend before. Two parties, and yours wins.
* Minus 1: Tell your family not to go. Nobody rewards that behavior with attendance.
* Plus 1: Let the party happen for your child's sake, and raise the scheduling move privately, once.
* Plus 2: Let it happen, and propose the standing rule that fixes the category: birthdays get planned together by a set date each year, alternating who hosts, so no parent ever gets ambushed by an invitation again.

Question 23. Two houses, two rulebooks
Bedtime and screens are strict at your house and wide open at the other parent's, and your kids have started playing the houses against each other.

* Minus 2: Tighten your rules further. If one house will not parent, the other has to parent double.
* Minus 1: Tell the kids exactly why the other house's rules are a problem. They are old enough to see it.
* Plus 1: Pick your battles: hold the two or three rules that protect sleep and school, and let the small differences be differences.
* Plus 2: Do that, and ask the other parent to align on just those two or three core rules in writing, framed around the kids' school performance, not around whose parenting is better.

Question 24. The class trip money
A school trip costs 300 dollars, due Friday. The other parent says trips are extra, support covers what it covers, and this one is on you.

* Minus 2: Pay it, and deduct 300 dollars from something you owe them. Balance restored.
* Minus 1: Pay it, and make sure your child knows who made the trip happen.
* Plus 1: Pay it so your child does not miss out, and raise the extra expenses question calmly afterward, in writing.
* Plus 2: Pay it, then propose a written extras rule you both use from now on: shared costs above a set amount get split, decided within 48 hours, so no child ever waits on a deadline while the adults argue.

Question 25. The morning after you lost it
Last night you lost your temper at exchange, raised your voice in front of the kids, and said things you cannot unsay. It is now the next morning.

* Minus 2: Nothing to fix. They pushed you there, and the kids saw the real situation for once.
* Minus 1: Send a long message explaining the pressure you are under, so your reaction gets understood in context.
* Plus 1: Send a short, plain message: "Last night was on me. I am sorry you and the kids saw that. It will not be the pattern."
* Plus 2: Send that, keep the exchange short and warm next time no matter what is said to you, and put your own rule in writing for yourself: if your voice starts rising, you say "let us finish this in writing" and walk to the car.

9. Safety note (appears on the intro page, and leads the Band 1 result)
A quiz score never makes anyone unsafe. Behavior does. If any of this is happening, treat it as the priority, ahead of any result: threats, physical harm, being followed or tracked without consent, money being controlled so you cannot leave, threats involving the kids, or fear that changes how you live in your own home.
If you are in immediate danger, call 911. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. If you can, talk to a trusted person, an attorney, or a local support organization before making moves that the other person could discover. Mediation is not the right first step where one person is afraid of the other. A consultation can help you sort out what is safe and what is not.
