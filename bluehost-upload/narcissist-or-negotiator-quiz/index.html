Can You Pass the Hostage Negotiator or Narcissist Test?

25 questions. One scale. Find out where your thinking lands, like a narcissist, or like a hostage negotiator.

Everybody says they want peace. Then the pressure hits, the phone lights up at 11 at night, and a different brain takes over.

One way of thinking treats every disagreement as a threat to the ego. It has to win, it keeps score, it punishes, and it would rather blow up a Tuesday than lose a point. The other way comes from hostage negotiation. It slows the moment down, listens for what the other person actually needs, and trades on interests instead of fighting over facts and positions. The exchange is about the interest, not the argument.

This quiz puts you in 25 real situations and asks what you would honestly do. Some are about divorce, custody, and co-parenting. Others are about work, money, family, neighbors, and business, because the same thinking shows up everywhere you have something to lose. At the end, a graph shows where your answers land on the scale, closer to narcissist thinking or closer to hostage negotiator thinking, and what to do with that either way.

It takes about 4 minutes.

Answer as yourself on a normal day, not your best day and not your worst hour. Pick the answer closest to what you would actually do, not what sounds good. There are no right answers, and trying to beat the quiz only blurs the one result that could help you. Your answers stay on your device.

Built for anyone in a situation of conflict or disagreement, with spouses, exes, co-parents, family, coworkers, neighbors, and business partners. Answer about yourself. If you are really here to check the other parent, be honest about that too, the result still teaches you the negotiator moves that work on that pattern.

Ego Is Driving

Your marker landed at the narcissist thinking end of the spectrum.

What this usually means: in your answers, the fight itself was running the decisions. Winning the point, protecting the ego, keeping score, and making sure the other parent felt the loss all ranked above the outcome your kids have to live with. In a divorce or custody case, that thinking is expensive in every currency: money, time, court patience, and your children's peace. Mediators see this range most often in cases that have been back to court again and again over the same issues.

The honest part: if you took this quiz about yourself and scored here, you just did something most people in this range never do. You answered honestly. That is the first negotiator move there is.

What to do next:

  1. Put a 12 hour rule on every heated reply. Write it, save it, send the calm version tomorrow. Half of this pattern lives in the first 10 minutes.
  2. Move every fight into writing, one topic per message, facts and dates only. Writing slows the ego down.
  3. Before your next move in the case, ask one question: "Does this get my kids a better schedule, or does it get me a better feeling?" If it is the feeling, it is the pattern talking.

Split Down the Middle

Your marker landed in the middle of the spectrum, leaning toward the fight.

What this usually means: you have negotiator instincts, and they hold on calm days. Under pressure, at night, at exchange, when money or the kids get used as leverage, the old pattern takes the wheel: the sharp reply, the scorekeeping, the threat answered with a threat. This is the most common range Sean sees, and it is the range where small changes pay off fastest, because you are not rebuilding your thinking, you are catching it earlier.

What to do next:

  1. Learn the label move from Question 16: "It sounds like..." followed by their feeling, not your verdict. It drops the temperature without giving up a single thing you need.
  2. Trade on interests, not positions. When you catch yourself defending a position ("she gets no extra day, ever"), ask what interest is under it, for you and for them. That is where agreements hide.
  3. Write your agreements down, specific and dated. Vague agreements are future fights, and the middle of this spectrum is where vague agreements go to restart the war.

Negotiator Lean

Your marker landed on the hostage negotiator side of the spectrum.

What this usually means: your answers slowed the moment down. You protected the kids from the middle of the fight, you answered proposals instead of accusations, and you reached for structure, writing, trades, and time, instead of punishment. In a custody case, this thinking settles cases. It also tends to be read well by mediators, evaluators, and judges, because it looks like what it is: a parent solving problems.

What to do next:

  1. Protect the lean. Your risk is not ego, it is exhaustion. One person negotiating calmly with a high conflict pattern can burn out and snap back. Written agreements and a mediator in the room are how you keep your side of the street clean without carrying the whole street.
  2. Put your good instincts into a real parenting plan: exchange windows, reply windows, holiday trades, a rule that money and court never travel through the kids. Your answers already described most of it.
  3. If the other parent will not meet you on this side of the spectrum, that is information, not failure. Structure and professional help exist for exactly that gap.

Hostage Negotiator Mode

Your marker landed deep on the hostage negotiator end of the spectrum.

What this usually means: across money, time, threats, and the kids, your answers consistently chose de-escalation, tactical empathy, written clarity, and your children's experience over your own scoreboard. That is rare, and in Sean's mediation room it is the profile of the parent whose case settles and stays settled. One caution from the negotiator world: calm is a skill, not a personality transplant. Keep the systems that produced these answers, especially sleep, written communication, and not negotiating at 11 p.m.

What to do next:

  1. Write it down while it is working. A clear parenting plan signed now protects your kids from the next hard season, when nobody is at their best.
  2. Share the approach, not the score. Telling the other parent your result will read as a victory lap. Showing up with the same calm, in writing, is what actually moves a case.
  3. If you are this steady and still stuck, the obstacle is structural, not personal. That is the exact situation mediation was built for.
