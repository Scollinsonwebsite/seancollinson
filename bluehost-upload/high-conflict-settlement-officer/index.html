What a high-conflict settlement officer does

Some disputes are not short on facts. They are short on trust. Custody battles, contested divorces, and business breakups can reach a point where every message escalates and every proposal is read as an attack. A settlement officer’s job is to change how the negotiation happens, so the people involved can reach terms they can live with.

Sean works as a neutral. He does not take sides, decide the outcome, represent either party, or give legal advice. He manages a structured, private process aimed at settlement, and the parties make the decisions.

How sessions work in high-conflict cases

  • Separate rooms when needed: sessions can be joint, fully separate, or a mix, with Sean carrying proposals between the parties.
  • Clear ground rules: how people speak to each other, how breaks work, and what happens if the conversation escalates.
  • A focused agenda: one issue at a time, starting where progress is most likely.
  • Pacing: enough time to think, without letting the case drift for months.
  • In person or by Zoom: virtual sessions use private breakout rooms, which many people find easier than sitting across the table.

Crisis negotiation skills, applied to settlement

Sean is an FBI-trained crisis negotiator who volunteers with the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department. Crisis negotiation is communication at its most demanding: active listening, emotional regulation, rapport, separating positions from interests, and turning those interests into specific, workable terms. Those are the skills high-conflict cases need most. Read about Sean’s background.

Working with attorneys

Attorneys are welcome to attend or to be available during sessions. When the parties reach agreement, the key terms are written down so counsel can review them and prepare final documents. In family matters, agreements then move through the court process that finalizes them. See divorce mediation and child custody mediation.

When this is not the right fit

A structured process depends on both parties being able to participate safely and freely. If there is a history of abuse, threats, or intimidation, mediation may not be appropriate, or may need special safeguards. Raise any safety concern during the consultation, and consider speaking with an attorney or advocate.

Questions about high-conflict settlement

How long does a high-conflict settlement take?

It depends on how many issues are in dispute, how entrenched the positions are, how complex the finances are, and how prepared both sides are. Some matters settle in a single session; others take several sessions over a few weeks. There is no fixed timeline, and no one can promise one.

How much does it cost?

Cost depends on the number of sessions and the preparation required. Mediation is often less expensive than contested litigation because it involves fewer hearings and less formal discovery, but every matter is different. Fees and payment terms are explained during the consultation.

What if we still can’t agree?

Even when a full settlement is not reached, a structured session often narrows the dispute to a handful of issues and makes any remaining litigation shorter and less expensive. Court remains available for anything that is not resolved.

Can a settlement be changed later?

It depends on the terms. In family matters, custody, parenting time, and child support can usually be revisited when circumstances change, while property division is generally final once a judgment is entered. Business settlements are generally binding once signed. Ask your attorney how this applies before you sign.
