High-Conflict Custody in Los Angeles: What Actually Works When You Can't Agree on Anything

By Sean Collinson, The Mediator — The Mediation Office of Sean CollinsonNovember 19, 2026

If you and your co-parent cannot get through a two-minute conversation about your own child without it turning into a fight, you already know the real problem. It is not the calendar. It is the pattern.

In my mediation room, I see the same loop every week. A simple text about pickup time turns into a thread that reads like evidence. An exchange in a parking lot becomes a scene your child watches from the back seat. Your kid starts carrying messages — and carrying the stress — between two homes. Both parents love their child. Neither one can get a sentence out without the old fight starting up again.

Here is what I tell them: you are not stuck because you are a bad parent, and you are not stuck because your co-parent is impossible. You are stuck because the pattern runs the conversation before either of you says a word. Patterns can be broken. But not the way most people try to break them.

Why court usually makes a high-conflict custody fight worse

Court is adversarial by design. Each side builds a case against the other. Positions harden, because backing down in litigation feels like losing. Everything you say and text becomes potential evidence, so every interaction gets colder and more hostile — which then becomes more evidence.

At the end of that process, a judge who has never met your child decides your holidays, your weekends, and your summer, based on a file. The whole thing is public record. And because court orders have to be written for the general case, they rarely fit a real life: a parent's rotating work schedule, Los Angeles traffic between two homes, a daughter's Saturday games, a son's therapy appointments.

None of this means court is never necessary. If there are genuine safety issues, court protection comes first, full stop. But for most high-conflict parents, the fight itself is the problem — and litigation feeds the fight. If you are looking for a mediator in Los Angeles because you want the fighting to stop rather than escalate, you are already thinking about this the right way.

What mediation does differently

Mediation is private, and the decisions stay with you. Nobody imposes a schedule on your family — you build one, with a neutral professional running the process so the conversation stays on the rails.

In a high-conflict case, the structure does the heavy lifting. We set ground rules. One person talks at a time. We take one issue at a time instead of re-arguing five years in one afternoon. If being in the same room is too much, we work from separate rooms or by Zoom, and I move between you. The focus stays on your child's actual week — school, activities, bedtime, homework — not on who was right in 2021.

This is the work I do as a child custody mediator in Los Angeles, and it is also where my other training comes in. I am an FBI-trained crisis negotiator. High emotion is not an obstacle to the process. It is what the process is built for.

The method: how a high-conflict custody case actually gets settled

Step 1 — Slow the room down. Nobody negotiates well when they are flooded. We lower the temperature first: ground rules, structured turns, breaks when they are needed. Calm is not a personality trait. It is a staircase, and you climb it one step at a time.

Step 2 — Separate the people from the problem. You are not there to solve each other. You are there to solve a schedule, a school decision, a holiday split. The moment we treat the problem as the opponent — instead of each other — the room changes.

Step 3 — Get under the position to the interest. "I want every Friday" is a position, and positions can only collide. Underneath it is usually an interest: "I coach his Saturday morning games, and I don't want to lose that." Interests can be traded, balanced, and built into a plan. Positions cannot.

Step 4 — Build it in detail, and put it in writing. Vague agreements are future fights. A workable plan names times, places, who drives, what happens when someone is running late, and how changes get requested. Detail is not bureaucracy. Detail is peace.

Step 5 — Reset, don't repeat. This is the step from my book, RESET OR REPEAT. When you feel the old loop starting — in a session, or in a text thread at 11pm — you have a choice: repeat the pattern, or reset. Pause. Breathe. Respond to what your child needs instead of reacting to what your co-parent said. One reset does not fix everything. But every agreement I have ever seen hold was built out of parents choosing the reset, one conversation at a time.

What a workable parenting plan actually needs

Whether your case is high-conflict or fairly calm, the plans that hold up cover the same ground:

  • The regular weekly schedule — where your child sleeps each night, in plain language.
  • Exchanges — where they happen, at what time, and who provides transportation.
  • Holidays, birthdays, and vacations — decided now, in writing, not negotiated in a panic the week before. This is where most plans fall apart, and it is why I work through holiday parenting schedules in detail.
  • Decision-making — how education, medical care, and activities get decided, and who can decide what alone in an emergency.
  • Communication rules — one channel, about the child, with reasonable response times. This single clause lowers the temperature of co-parenting more than almost anything else.
  • A change process — life changes. The plan should say how you revisit it.
  • A disagreement clause — when something new comes up, you come back to mediation before anyone files anything.

When the conflict is at a ten

Some parents cannot be in a room together, and some cases involve attorneys, big money already spent, and positions locked hard. That is still mediable — it just needs a different structure: separate sessions, a mediator who will manage the strategy of the conversation, and sometimes a parental coordinator for the parenting plan itself. That is the high-conflict settlement officer work I do for families and for attorneys whose cases have stalled. You do not have to be able to sit together to settle. You only have to be willing to stop repeating.

Frequently asked questions

Can custody mediation work if we can't be in the same room?
Yes. Mediation does not require you to sit together. Sessions can run with each parent in a separate room while the mediator moves between you, or by Zoom. The structure is built for exactly that situation.

What if my co-parent won't compromise on anything?
You do not need to agree on everything, or even like each other, to settle a parenting plan. Mediation works on one issue at a time and focuses on your child's needs and each parent's real interests underneath the positions. Most high-conflict parents are surprised by what moves once the conversation is structured.

How long does custody mediation take compared to going to court?
Court custody cases commonly run through multiple hearings spread over months, on the court's schedule. Mediation is scheduled around your life and usually works through a parenting plan in a small number of focused sessions. Every case is different, and timing is covered in the consultation.

Is a mediated parenting plan enforceable?
Yes. Once your agreement is written up, signed, and filed with and approved by the court, it becomes a court order with the same force as one a judge imposed — except you built it yourselves, around your child's actual life.

How much does child custody mediation cost?
Cost depends on the number of sessions and the preparation your case needs, and fees are explained fully in the consultation before you commit to anything. What mediation does not carry is the cost structure of two attorneys litigating against each other through repeated court hearings.
